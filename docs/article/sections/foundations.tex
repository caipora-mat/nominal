\section{Finite permutations and actions}
\label{sec:foundations}

Let $A$ be an arbitrary atom carrier. Neither infinitude nor countability
is needed for the permutation and action theory below. Atom and value
carriers may inhabit independent Lean universes,
$A:\texttt{Type}\ u$, $X:\texttt{Type}\ v$ and $Y:\texttt{Type}\ w$.
The central distinction is between the finite set moved by a permutation
and the support of a value under a selected action.

\subsection{Finite-moved permutations}
\label{sec:permutations}

\begin{definition}
For a bijection $\pi:A\to A$, let
$\moved(\pi)=\{a\in A\mid\pi(a)\ne a\}$.
The group $\Perm(A)$ consists of bijections with finite moved-point sets,
with multiplication $(\pi\sigma)(a)=\pi(\sigma(a))$.
\end{definition}

Lean represents \texttt{Perm A} as a subgroup of \texttt{Equiv.Perm A}.
Finiteness is a proof field: applying a permutation
requires neither a finite enumeration nor a choice of support. Equality
is extensional, and the coercion to bijections respects the group operations.

\begin{proposition}[Moved-point laws]
\label{prop:subgroup}
Finite-moved permutations form a subgroup, and
\begin{align}
  \moved(1)&=\varnothing,\label{eq:moved-one}\\
  \moved(\pi\sigma)&\subseteq\moved(\pi)\cup\moved(\sigma),
    \label{eq:moved-mul}\\
  \moved(\pi^{-1})&=\moved(\pi),\label{eq:moved-inverse}\\
  \moved(\pi\sigma\pi^{-1})&=\pi[\moved(\sigma)].
    \label{eq:moved-conj}
\end{align}
Here $\pi[S]$ denotes direct image.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A point fixed by both factors is fixed by their product, and a bijection
and its inverse fix the same points. These observations give subgroup
closure. For conjugation, injectivity of $\pi$ gives
$(\pi\sigma\pi^{-1})(a)\ne a$ exactly when
$\sigma(\pi^{-1}(a))\ne\pi^{-1}(a)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Transpositions and controlled factorization}
\label{sec:swaps}
\label{sec:controlled-swaps}

Write $\swap a b$ for the transposition of $a$ and $b$, fixing every other
atom. The Lean operation \texttt{Perm.swap} uses decidable equality on
$A$, which classical reasoning can supply locally. The permutation group
itself requires no such assumption.

\begin{proposition}[Swap equations]
\label{prop:swaps}
A swap exchanges its endpoints, fixes all other atoms, and satisfies
\[
  \swap a a=1,\qquad \swap a b^{-1}=\swap a b,\qquad
  \pi\swap a b\pi^{-1}=\swap{\pi(a)}{\pi(b)}.
\]
If $a\ne b$, its moved-point set is $\{a,b\}$.
\end{proposition}

These identities follow by evaluating at the endpoints and at points
outside them. In particular, a swap is its own inverse, so its square is
the identity. The conjugation identity also holds when the endpoints
coincide.

Swap generation alone is insufficient for support arguments: the factors
must avoid specified atoms. For a finite list
$l=[(a_1,b_1),\ldots,(a_n,b_n)]$, put
\[
  W(l)=\swap{a_1}{b_1}\cdots\swap{a_n}{b_n},\qquad W([])=1.
\]
The rightmost factor acts first. In Lean, $W(l)$ is the ordinary product
of the list obtained by mapping \texttt{Perm.swap} over the endpoint pairs.

\begin{proposition}[Factorization within the original moved set]
\label{prop:controlled-factorization}
For every $\pi\in\Perm(A)$ there is a finite list $l$ with $W(l)=\pi$
such that every $(a,b)\in l$ satisfies
\[
  a\ne b,\qquad a\in\moved(\pi),\qquad b\in\moved(\pi).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $M=\moved(\pi)$ and, in the group of all bijections of $A$, let
$G_M=\{\swap a b\mid a\ne b,\ a,b\in M\}$.
Mathlib's restricted-transposition closure theorem characterizes membership
of $\sigma$ in the subgroup generated by $G_M$: its moved-point set must
be finite, and $\sigma(a)$ must lie in the orbit of $a$ under that subgroup
for every $a$.

These conditions hold for $\pi$. Finiteness is part of its certificate.
If $\pi(a)=a$, the identity witnesses the orbit condition. Otherwise
$a,\pi(a)\in M$: injectivity shows that $\pi(a)$ cannot be fixed unless
$a$ is fixed. Thus the generator $\swap a{\pi(a)}$ sends $a$ to $\pi(a)$.
Left closure induction now extracts a list: the identity gives the empty
list, and multiplication by a generator or its inverse prepends the same
endpoint pair, since swaps are self-inverse. Every endpoint remains in
the fixed set $M$ of the original permutation.
\end{proof}

The theorem is \path{Perm.swap_factorization}; the proof uses Mathlib's
\path{mem_closure_isSwap} and \path{Subgroup.closure_induction_left}.
The finite descent underlying the closure theorem is instructive. If
$\sigma(a)\ne a$, then $\rho=\swap a{\sigma(a)}\sigma$ fixes $a$ and
\[
  \moved(\rho)\subseteq\moved(\sigma)\setminus\{a\},\qquad
  |\moved(\rho)|<|\moved(\sigma)|.
\]
This is a strict decrease, not necessarily deletion of exactly one point:
correcting a two-cycle removes both endpoints. Restricting the generators
to $G_M$ preserves the original-set bound throughout the argument.

\begin{proposition}[Factorization avoiding a pointwise fixed set]
\label{prop:avoidance-factorization}
If $\pi$ fixes an arbitrary $S\subseteq A$ pointwise, there is a list
$l$ with $W(l)=\pi$ whose endpoint pairs satisfy
$a\ne b$, $a\notin S$ and $b\notin S$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every moved atom lies outside $S$. Apply
Proposition~\ref{prop:controlled-factorization}.
\end{proof}

Neither $S$ nor its complement needs to be finite. Both factorization
results apply to finite, empty and singleton atom carriers. They assert
existence, without specifying a canonical or shortest factorization.

\subsection{Selected actions}
\label{sec:actions}

An action on $X$ is an operation $\pi\act x$ satisfying
$1\act x=x$ and $(\pi\sigma)\act x=\pi\act(\sigma\act x)$.
The system uses Mathlib's \texttt{MulAction (Perm A) X} directly.
Consequently, a theorem about support or equivariance refers to the selected
action on its carrier; changing that action changes the mathematical input.

The canonical atom action is evaluation, $\pi\act a=\pi(a)$.
Products use the componentwise action, and finite atom sets use direct image:
\begin{gather*}
  \pi\act(x,y)=(\pi\act x,\pi\act y),\qquad \pi\act S=\pi[S],\\
  a\in\pi\act S\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \pi^{-1}(a)\in S.
\end{gather*}
The atom action restricts Mathlib's bijection action to the subgroup;
the product and finite-set actions likewise reuse standard constructions.
The finite-set action is scoped by \texttt{Pointwise} in Lean.

\subsection{Invariance tested by swaps}
\label{sec:swap-invariance}

\begin{proposition}[Pointwise-fixer invariance and outside swaps]
\label{prop:swap-invariance}
For any selected action on $X$, arbitrary $S\subseteq A$, and $x\in X$,
\begin{align*}
  &\bigl(\forall\pi\in\Perm(A),\
      (\forall c\in S,\ \pi(c)=c)\Longrightarrow\pi\act x=x\bigr)\\
  &\hspace{2em}\Longleftrightarrow
    \bigl(\forall a,b\notin S,\ \swap a b\act x=x\bigr).
\end{align*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A permutation fixing $S$ pointwise factors into swaps outside $S$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:avoidance-factorization}. If each factor fixes $x$,
the action laws show that their product does too. Conversely, a swap with
both endpoints outside $S$ fixes $S$ pointwise.
\end{proof}

This is \path{Perm.forall_smul_eq_iff_swap_smul_eq}. Equal endpoints are
allowed because $\swap a a=1$. No finite-support or infinitude assumption
enters the equivalence. Pointwise fixation is essential: setwise
preservation $\pi[S]=S$ permits permutations among the members of $S$.

\subsection{Discrete data and equivariant maps}
\label{sec:discrete}
\label{sec:equivariance}

\begin{definition}[Discrete permutation set]
The discrete permutation set $\Discrete_A(X)$ is a copy of $X$ with trivial
action $\pi\act\operatorname{mk}(x)=\operatorname{mk}(x)$.
\end{definition}

This is the discrete group action of Pitts~\cite[Example~1.5, p.~15]{pitts2013}
and, for infinite atoms, a discrete nominal set
\cite[Example~2.5, p.~30]{pitts2013}. It treats data as unaffected by atom
renaming: numerical annotations are one example, but even an atom can be
carried as a fixed label. The construction forgets any action on $X$;
it is neither the subset of fixed points nor an orbit quotient.

Lean represents \texttt{Discrete A X} by a structure with one field
\texttt{val : X}. The atom parameter fixes the acting group without storing
a value or raising the carrier universe. The distinct type
allows its trivial action to coexist with an action on $X$, at the cost
of explicit constructor and projection conversions. A definitionally equal
type tag is another possible representation, but action selection then
requires attention to the type inferred for each expression. The type
equivalence $\Discrete_A(X)\simeq X$ alone does not identify the actions.

Every discrete value has singleton orbit and empty least support: all
permutations fix it, and the empty set is contained in every supporting set.
The canonical support interface and its atom assumptions are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:canonical-support}.

\begin{definition}
A function $f:X\to Y$ between selected actions is \emph{equivariant} when
\[
  \Equivariant_A(f)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \forall\pi\in\Perm(A)\ \forall x\in X,\quad
  f(\pi\act x)=\pi\act f(x).
\]
\end{definition}

The Lean predicate \texttt{Equivariant A f} leaves $f$ an ordinary
function and takes the atom type explicitly. It requires no action on the
function type itself.

\begin{proposition}[Elementary equivariant maps]
\label{prop:equivariant}
Identity maps and product projections are equivariant. Equivariant maps
are closed under composition and pairing.
\end{proposition}

The claims follow by composing the action equations and applying them
componentwise. Fixing a parameter in a jointly equivariant operation,
however, need not leave an equivariant function: the joint equation
renames the parameter as well as the argument.

\subsection{Three distinctions between actions}
\label{sec:counterexamples}

Let $a\ne b$ and $\tau=\swap a b$. The following examples explain why
specifying the action is part of specifying the object under study.

\begin{example}[An atom and a discrete copy]
\label{ex:discrete}
The canonical action moves $a$, since $\tau\act a=b\ne a$.
The discrete action fixes $\operatorname{mk}(a)$.
Consequently, $\operatorname{val}:\Discrete_A(A)\to A$ is a bijection of
types but is not equivariant: applying it after renaming gives $a$, whereas
renaming its value gives $b$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Left multiplication and conjugation]
\label{ex:group-actions}
A group acts on itself by left multiplication, $\pi\act\sigma=\pi\sigma$,
and by conjugation, $\pi\star\sigma=\pi\sigma\pi^{-1}$. At the identity,
\[
  \tau\act1=\tau\ne1,\qquad \tau\star1=1.
\]
The bare group $\Perm(A)$ retains its standard left action. The moved-point
conjugation law~\eqref{eq:moved-conj} is a group equation; it does not select
conjugation as the group's scalar action.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Pointwise and conjugation actions on functions]
\label{ex:function-actions}
For $f:X\to Y$, the pointwise action uses only the codomain action,
whereas conjugation also renames the argument:
\[
  (\pi\act_{\mathrm{pt}}f)(x)=\pi\act f(x),\qquad
  (\pi\act_{\mathrm{conj}}f)(x)=\pi\act f(\pi^{-1}\act x).
\]
On the identity function of the canonical atom carrier,
\[
  (\tau\act_{\mathrm{pt}}\id_A)(a)=b,\qquad
  (\tau\act_{\mathrm{conj}}\id_A)(a)=a.
\]
Equivariance is precisely invariance under conjugation: substituting
$\pi^{-1}\act x$ in the equivariance equation gives the fixed-point
equation, and substituting $\pi\act x$ gives the converse. It is not
invariance under the pointwise action, which Mathlib assigns to bare
function types. Nor does an arbitrary function necessarily have finite
support under conjugation.
\end{example}
